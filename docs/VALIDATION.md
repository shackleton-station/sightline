# Validation

All numbers come from `web/public/data/<site>/validation.json`, regenerated by `scripts/run_all.py`.

**Bottom line:** the Sun and Earth positions match JPL Horizons essentially exactly, and terrain heights match
NASA's published landing-site elevations. Across ordinary polar ground, the model agrees with LROC's map from real photos
within about 3 points, and calls dated photos lit or dark correctly 99% of the time (75 to 99 if the line between them moves; §11). At sites away from ridges, Sun and Earth visibility agree with NASA's maps within 3.5 points. On
our four ridge-top sites, though, our Sun values run 3 to 24 points **above** NASA's map at 2 m, and within 100 m a month's
Sun varies by 20 to 68 points (§11). More calculation won't improve this; location and terrain decide it.

The Atlas tab's map of every 120 m near the pole is the same calculation run a million times: it matches the site tracks
hour for hour at the sites' own points, and NASA's map within 1.2 points on average across the square (§12).

So what can you rely on?

- **Comparing months at one site:** most reliable, since a site's offset is likely to carry across months. That's
  an expectation, not something we've tested.
- **Comparing two sites:** weaker. The offset differs by site, so a gap of up to about 21 points in Sun could come
  from the model. The comparison table accounts for this and says "Too close".
- **A single site's percentage:** treat it as model output.

## 1. Sun and Earth positions vs JPL Horizons

40 checks at each of 13 sites (Sun and Earth at 5 weekly timestamps, in May of 2026, 2027, 2030 and 2049: the two ends of the
data and the two ends of the four years it first covered). Largest separation at any site:
**0.00002°** (target: about 0.1°). Responses are cached and tests use a recorded one, so CI makes no API calls.

**Every hour, as shipped.** `pipeline/scripts/compare_horizons.py` asks Horizons for the Sun and Earth at every hour of
2026 to 2049 at each of the 13 sites (5,469,984 comparisons, ephemeris DE441) and compares them with the
angles the web app ships, which are rounded to 0.01°. Largest gap at any site: **0.0071°**; typical (median) gap:
0.003° to 0.004°. That is the rounding itself (two angles, each up to 0.005° off), so what a visitor reads on screen can
be reproduced in Horizons to the last digit shown. Per site: `web/public/data/horizons.json`. One request at a time, a
pause between them, replies kept in `cache/horizons-hourly/`, as JPL's fair-use policy asks. The numbers for 2027 to 2030
alone were the same (911,664 comparisons, largest gap 0.0071°), so the agreement does not drift over the 24 years.
`--sample` is the quick version, for after the range changes: about one hour a month across every year, 26 requests in all
(7,410 comparisons on 2026-11-13, largest gap 0.0070°).

NASA's maps don't reach Apollo, Blue Ghost, SLIM or Chang'e 4, so these sites have no independent check on
visibility. Over 18.6 years, they still behave as physics says they should:

## 8. 3D view vs the terrain and the model

> **2026-11-11: the 3D view now uses Three.js** (directional sunlight with cascaded shadow maps,
> `web/src/three/TerrainViewer.ts`) instead of the ray-traced shader measured below. It was measured again on
> 2026-11-12: see [The Three.js view](#the-threejs-view-measured-2026-11-12) at the end of this section. It
> agrees less well than the old view did, because it misses shadows cast by nearby terrain. The figures between
> here and there describe the old view and are kept as history. The old shader also left all ground beyond the
> 8 km fine square dark: the coarser levels are drawn 15 m and 80 m lower so the levels don't overlap, and the shader
> traced sunlight from those lowered points against the real heights, so every one started underground. This
> check sampled only within about 7.5 km, so it didn't catch that. Inside 8 km, the new view lights the same
> ground as the old one (compared by eye at Connecting Ridge, 2027-05-01 00:00 UTC, from above and straight down).
> The numbers on the page never came from the picture; they still come from the model.

Checked on 2026-11-03, on one Mac GPU in Chrome, with a one-off browser script that isn't in the repo or CI. All
hours are in May 2027.

The test: pick random ground points within about 7.5 km of a site, trace a ray toward the Sun through the LOLA
heights in dense fixed steps, and compare that with the color of the pixel drawn there. Points facing away from
the Sun or inside the Sun's disk edge are left out, because they can't give a yes or no.

| Compared at 5,752 points, 26 hours, 7 sites | Agreement |
| --- | --- |
| **Final 3D view vs ray-traced terrain** | **98.1%** (93 drawn lit but shadowed, 14 the reverse) |
| Cesium version, 4,000 points, 20 hours, 6 sites, for comparison | 38.9% (2,412 drawn lit but shadowed, 32 the reverse) |

Agreement alone is flattering, because many hours are nearly all lit or all dark. The 7 hours with at least 20 lit
and 20 shadowed points can tell a right Sun from a wrong one:

| 1,414 points in those 7 hours | Agreement |
| --- | --- |
| Actual Sun direction | **97.2%** |
| Sun rotated 90° | 46.5% |
| Sun flipped 180° | 33.3% |

**The ray tracing itself vs the model:** every 6th hour at each of the 7 sites, from 2 m above the ground, it gave
the same Sun up or blocked answer as our track in 867 of 868 hours. Where the model has the Sun fully up or fully
blocked, that's 786 of 786. Its one difference is an hour where the Sun is partly hidden.

**Direction of the Sun:** a test in `web/src/lib/moon3d.test.ts` checks that the vector lighting the view matches
the panorama's Sun within 0.05°, at 5 sites for every hour of May.

### Limits of this check

- It's about the ground. The numbers on the page are for a point 2 m up, and at ridge sites the Sun is often below
  the local horizontal, so the ground can be dark while the numbers correctly say the Sun is up. The view says so.
- It inherits our terrain and model, including the ridge gap against PGDA in §2. Agreeing with the model doesn't
  make the model right.
- The view casts shadows only from terrain within 250 km (200 km on small screens), against 200 km in the model.
- Small screens use coarser terrain and fewer ray steps. That setting wasn't part of this test.
- The blue tint on unlit ground is a display choice. So is the brightness of lit ground: only lit or unlit is checked.

### The Three.js view (measured 2026-11-12)

Measured with `web/scripts/shadow-check.mjs`, which is kept so anyone can run it again
(`cd web && node scripts/shadow-check.mjs`; it isn't in CI). It ran in headless Chrome 157 on one Mac (Apple
M4 Max, macOS 27.2), drawn by the GPU through Metal, not in software. All hours are in May 2027.

The test is the one above, on the view's own code. The page draws `TerrainViewer` from 20 km straight above the
site (a 1600 × 1600 px picture) with the Sun direction the app uses for that hour. It picks 250 random ground
points within 7.5 km. For each it steps along the line to the Sun through the LOLA heights (every 20 m out to
40 km, then every 200 m out to 250 km) and compares that with the pixel drawn there.

- **Sites and hours:** all 13 sites. At each, four hours spread evenly through the part of May when the Sun's
  center is higher than 3° below level, plus the first and last hours with the Sun 2° to 6° up, where there are
  any. That gives 70 hours and 17,500 points. The rule picks the hours before anything is drawn.
- **Left out:** 3,834 points whose slope faces away from the Sun, and 499 where the horizon cuts the Sun's disk
  (the terrain comes within the Sun's radius, 0.267°, of the line to its center). That leaves 13,167 points:
  10,453 lit and 2,714 shadowed.
- **Reading a pixel:** every hour is also drawn with the Sun straight below, which gives each pixel's unlit
  color. A point's sunlight is how much brighter it is drawn than that, so the blue tint and the brightness of
  lit ground don't come into it. A point counts as drawn lit when it has more than half the full sunlight for
  its slope. Full sunlight is read from the picture itself: the light gained for each unit of slope toward the
  Sun, taking the middle value over all points. The stricter column counts any sunlight at all as lit.

fully blocked, that's 1,520 of 1,521.

Limits of this measurement:

- The first two limits above still apply: it's about the ground, not a point 2 m up, and it inherits our terrain
  and model.
- One camera: straight down from 20 km. The shadow maps follow the camera, so other views can differ (the app
  opens 30 km away, looking down at 30°). Not measured.
- One machine and one GPU. Shadow maps can come out differently on other graphics hardware, and no phone was
  tested. The small-screen rows use that setting's coarser terrain and shadow maps on the same Mac and the same
  1600 px picture, not a phone's screen.
- The view treats the Sun as a point. Points where that matters are the ones left out, so this says nothing
  about how partly lit ground is drawn.
- Away from the south pole our fine terrain is about 237 m per sample, so those sites have few small shadows to
  get wrong. 14 of the 18 mixed hours are at the six sites south of 80°S.
- The view casts shadows from terrain within 250 km (200 km on the small-screen setting); the stepping looks
  250 km along the line.

## Why the ridge sites may differ

These are plausible causes. The non-ridge results above support the first, but none has been tested directly:

- **Height on a ridge:** at these sites, Sun moves 16 to 28 points between 0 m and 2 m, much more than typical
  terrain. If PGDA's maps are effectively at surface level, a 2 m observer on a ridge top would read higher.
- **Different terrain models:** PGDA's map files are dated 2016-08 at 60 m/px; our DEM is the 2023 product at
  20 m/px. Coarser terrain can blunt the peaks that catch the most light.
- **Point vs pixel:** we compute one point, while PGDA reports a 60 m pixel. Neighbours differ by up to 56
  points (Shackleton Rim). A later test suggests this isn't the main cause (§10).

Two outside references, for context only:

- The region files we took the sites from are named `regionProps_avgillGT070`, which suggests NASA's own later
  5 m analysis found more than 70% average illumination there. That would put three of the four PGDA map pixels
  (58–68%) too low, and our 0 m and 2 m values on either side. This reading of the filename is unconfirmed.
- Gläser et al. (2014) report locations on Connecting Ridge lit 92.3% of the time at 2 m, close to our 90.28%
  (any part). Their location isn't confirmed to be ours.

## Not checked

- The 18.6-year periods may differ: PGDA doesn't state its exact dates.
- One PGDA pixel near Connecting Ridge reads 0% in both maps while its neighbours read 26–86% (Sun) and 41–58%
  (Earth). It looks like a map defect but is unconfirmed. It isn't in the 3×3 neighbourhood above.

## 9. The Sun on Earth

The app compares the Sun at a lunar site with the Sun on Earth (`earth_sun.py`, `web/src/lib/earthSun.ts`). SPICE gives
the point with the Sun overhead each hour, in the `IAU_EARTH` frame, and one formula turns that into the Sun's height at
any place.

**Checked against JPL Horizons:** 45 timestamps from 2027 to 2030, at 40°N, the equator and 89.5°S, with Horizons' airless
apparent elevation. Largest difference **0.094°**.

- **Where it comes from:** about 0 at the pole and about 0.08° at the equator, changing sign between the Sun rising and
  setting. So latitude is right and time is off by about 20 seconds, as expected from `IAU_EARTH`, a low-precision model
  of Earth's rotation. We didn't add high-precision Earth orientation kernels, because this is invisible on a chart
  spanning 120°, and within the ~0.1° expected in `CLAUDE.md`.
- **What it leaves out:** the atmosphere, hills and buildings. Refraction lifts the Sun about 0.5° near the horizon, so real
  sunsets on Earth come a few minutes later. The app says so next to the chart.

### What this adds on the ridge gap

Random well-lit pixels near each ridge site read within about 3 points on average (−3.1 to +2.9), while the sites themselves read 3 to 24 high (§2).

### Dated LROC photos

A sharper test than the map: was the ground lit at the moment each photo was taken? `scripts/check_lroc_photos.py` reads 20 pixels from 235 single photos taken between February 2010 and October 2011, then compares each with the model at that time. It uses ranged requests, so it reads about 19 KB in all instead of 35 GB of whole frames. It isn't part of `run_all.py`, because it depends on a remote archive that often says to slow down. Results are in `lroc-photos.json`.

**How photos are called lit or dark.** Across ordinary ground, brightness splits into two groups, with few values between 0.001 and 0.003. Ridge pixels don't split like that: a fifth to two fifths of their photos fall in between, perhaps because part of the 100 m pixel is lit or the Sun is very low (we haven't tested why). So we score them three ways, none of which uses the model to choose the line:

- **Clear photos only:** below 0.001 is dark and 0.003 or above is lit, leaving out the rest. The limits come from the split on ordinary ground, but they were chosen after seeing the first scores, so treat this as the most favorable reading.
- **Every photo, split at 0.00288:** the line where each pixel's lit share best matches LROC's own map (within 3.9 points on average).
- **Every photo, at five other lines,** from 0.0005 to 0.005.

**The control** is the same test with the Sun 14.77 days out of step. It keeps the season but puts the Sun on the other side of the sky. Where ground is lit from many directions, it can score well without knowing the Sun's direction, so a high control means the test says less there.

Scores count lit and dark photos equally, so 50 is what a coin flip gets. Brackets show the middle 95% when frames are redrawn at random.

**What this doesn't show: the Sun at 2 m,** which is the number the app leads with. The photos show the ground. Scoring the 2 m model against them anyway gives 80 to 84 on clear photos at the sites and 60 to 88 across the lines, but a point 2 m up can really be lit while the ground below is dark, so these scores can't count for or against it. On the 8 well-lit spots the 2 m model scores 94.9 on clear photos, with 35.8 for the control.

Frames were drawn at random from equal slices of time, by taking the first frame after each random time, so a frame after a gap in coverage is slightly more likely to be picked. Frame names give only the minute a frame started, and each takes about 6.5 minutes, so times are good to about 4 minutes; the Sun moves about 0.03° in that time. About a tenth of the pixels are blank, up to 17% at the sites, and those are left out. All from 2010–11, not the months the app shows.

### A real sunset

Blue Ghost Mission 1, in Mare Crisium: the model has Sun at touchdown, and its last sunset on 16 March 2025 between 19:45 UTC (center of the disk) and 20:18 UTC (last of the disk), with Earth in view throughout. Press reports put the last signal, at 23:15 UTC, about five hours after sunset, which implies about 18:15 UTC. So the model is 1.5 to 2 hours later than a rough figure, at a site where our terrain is 237 m per pixel. This is too loose to count for or against.

## 12. The light grid: every 120 m near the pole, every 300 m to 85° S (2026-11-12)

The Atlas tab draws Sun and Earth visibility for every pixel of a 1,024 × 1,024 grid of 120 m pixels centred on the south pole
(61 km each way). It is the same calculation as a listed site, at each pixel's centre, 2 m up: a horizon of 720 azimuths out to
200 km on the 20 m terrain, then every hour of 2027 to 2030 (`pipeline/src/clps/lightgrid.py`, `scripts/make_light_grid.py`).

**Bottom line:** the grid's code gives exactly the site pipeline's answer at the sites' own coordinates, and across a million
pixels its long-run averages are within 1.2 points of NASA's map on average. On well-lit ground the check says little, as
everywhere else in this document. A tapped pixel is one point: the pixel holding a ridge site differs from the site, 8 to 71 m
away, in 0.4 to 8.5% of hours, and by up to 30 points in its worst month.

A second, wider grid (300 m pixels out to 85° S) surrounds this one. It has its own section below, with its own numbers:
they are a little worse, and a 300 m pixel stands for its ground less well than a 120 m one.

### The file, the reader and the picture

- **Packing.** A month stores, for each pixel, the hours at which the Sun and Earth change: up to 16 for the Sun and 4 for
  Earth. A pixel with more has its shortest runs merged. With 16, that touches 0.019% of pixel-months and changes one hour
  in two million; Earth never needed more than 4. The first version kept 8, and a test caught it changing 13 of a month's
  744 hours in a listed site's pixel: on ridge tops a grazing Sun comes and goes, and those are the pixels that matter.
- **Reader.** A web test (`web/src/lib/lightGrid.test.ts`) requires the browser's code to find the same pixel as Python for
  each ridge site, and to read that pixel's May 2027 exactly as Python counted it before packing: every hour, and the
  percentages and longest gaps.
- **Picture.** The drawn map was compared with the file in 16 pictures (the four ridge sites at four hours of May 2027, at
  the site zoom, in headless Chrome). At 1,894,556 pixel centres on screen the picture is lit or dark as the file says at
  all but 249, and at all but 29 of the 1,819,763 whose neighbours agree with them (the rest are edges, where the picture
  blends). Most ground is dark, so a wrong map agrees often too: the same pictures against the file half a lunar day out of
  step, upside down, mirrored, transposed, or with Earth's flags agree at 70 to 88%. One-off script, not in the repo.
- **Speed.** `web/scripts/atlas-frames.mjs` times each frame's work until the graphics card has finished it, in headless
  Chrome at 1440 × 900 with two device pixels per CSS pixel (Apple M4 Max, Metal, the development build). Scrubbing a new
  hour every frame, React's re-render included: median 6.0 ms, 95th percentile 7.0, worst 7.4. Flying between zooms:
  median 5.8, worst 6.7. Playing the month: median 4.7, 95th 5.6, with one frame of 18.6 ms in 121. Nothing is drawn while
  nothing changes, and no frame kept the page busy over 50 ms. On the live site's production build, same machine, the
  same day: scrubbing median 5.5 ms, 95th 5.8, worst 6.6; flying median 6.2, worst 7.4; playing median 5.0, with one frame
  of 14.9 ms in 125. Not measured on a phone or a slower laptop. With the wider grid drawn as well (three more textures, the
  development build): scrubbing median 6.0 ms, 95th 6.7, worst 7.5; flying median 5.3, worst 6.7; playing median 4.4,

### Limits

- **A pixel is one point, 2 m above its centre,** not an average of its 120 m. "Sun in view" means any part of the disk
  from there; the ground beneath can be dark (§8, limits).
- **The Sun and Earth are placed from the Moon's centre** (light time taken there, each pixel's own position subtracted),
  the same shortcut as the nearby maps. At the four sites it changed no hour against the per-observer calculation.
- **The relief inside the square is a drawing aid.** Hills are shaded from the Sun's real compass direction as if it stood
  30° high, so they read in light and in shadow. Only lit or dark is data, and the darkest lit ground is still drawn more
  than twice as bright as the brightest unlit ground.
- **Outside the wider square (beyond 85° S at its edges) nothing is computed.** The map there is the dimmed slope
  shading of the Scout tab.
- **Between the two squares a pixel is 300 m,** and one point 2 m above its centre stands for it. Sharp ridges narrower
  than that are missed or overstated; the 120 m grid covers the ridges the listed sites sit on, except IM-2 Athena's.
- It inherits everything above: the ridge-top gap against NASA's map (§2), and the model's limits on well-lit ground.

## 13. The Surface view: the picture against the track (2026-11-12)

The Surface tab (`/surface/`) draws what the pipeline computed as a view from the site: the land is a shape whose
top edge is `horizon.json` (the ground drawn under that edge is an illustration, and is not checked here), and the Sun and Earth are disks at the track's azimuth and elevation, hidden by the land in
front of them. Nothing new is computed for it. This section asks one thing: **does the picture show what the track
says?** The geometry is plain functions (`web/src/lib/surfaceView.ts`), so it is checked without a graphics card, and
the Three.js view sets its camera from the same functions.

### What is compared

- **The size of the disks.** The track has directions but no distances. The radius is worked out as the pipeline does
  (`visibility.py`: the angle whose sine is the body's radius over its distance), from the ephemeris files the pipeline
  already writes for custom coordinates (`data/custom/ephemeris-<year>.bin`: the Sun's and Earth's positions from the
  Moon's center each hour). The site is put on the 1,737.4 km sphere; its real height changes Earth's size by under one
  part in 10,000. In May 2027 the Sun's radius runs from 0.2626° to 0.2649° and Earth's from 0.90° to 1.00°. As a check
  on these radii: put into the pipeline's own rule (a level skyline under the disk's middle) they give the track's
  `sun_frac` to within 0.022 in every hour of May 2027 at all 13 sites, which is the rounding of the track's angles to 0.01°.
- **The share of the Sun's disk above the land.** The view follows the skyline's shape across the disk (the horizon
  file's points joined by straight lines). The track's figure treats the skyline as level, at its height under the
  disk's middle. So the two are not the same number, and should differ only where the land slopes under the Sun.
- **Any part showing.** Whether any point of the disk's rim stands above the skyline, found exactly for that shape,
  against the track's `sun_up` and `earth_up`.

### What was measured

`web/src/lib/surfaceView.test.ts`, in the test suite, for every hour of May 2027 at three ridges:

| Site | Hours | Share of the Sun's disk: worst difference | On average | "Any part showing" differs: Sun | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Connecting Ridge | 744 | 0.023 | 0.0015 | 2 hours | 0 |
| Shackleton Rim | 744 | 0.030 | 0.0015 | 1 hour | 0 |
| de Gerlache Rim | 744 | 0.054 | 0.0008 | 0 | 0 |

The stated tolerance is **0.06 of the disk** in any hour, and the test fails beyond it. The three hours that differ are
12 May 21:00 and 22 May 03:00 at Connecting Ridge (the track says up; the rim is 0.0009° and 0.0020° under the skyline)
and 10 May 20:00 at Shackleton Rim (the track says hidden; the rim is 0.0024° over it). The track and the horizon file
are each rounded to 0.01°, so a rim within 0.01° of the skyline can read either way: the pipeline decided from the
unrounded angles.

The same functions over the whole data set (`SURFACE_FULL=1 npx vitest run src/lib/surfaceView.test.ts`): all 13 sites,
all 48 months, 455,832 hours.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Share of the Sun's disk, view against track | differs by 0.00024 on average, 0.054 at worst |
| "Any part showing", the Sun | differs in 110 hours: 102 within the 0.01° rounding, 8 where a sliver shows beside the disk's middle |
| "Any part showing", Earth | differs in 95 hours: 66 within the rounding, 29 a sliver beside the middle |

Every one of the 205 is one of those two kinds; the test stops on any hour it cannot explain. The second kind is real,
and small: the track says hidden because the skyline under the disk's middle covers it, while the skyline dips to one
side far enough to show an edge (at most 0.055° of rim; under 1% of the Sun's disk). It can only go that way round: over
the skyline's true shape the rim never stands lower than over a level line under its middle, and the test checks that
in every hour.

**What the page does about it.** The track decides. A body the track counts as hidden is not drawn (it is outlined, with
"behind the land"), so the picture never shows a sliver the answer beside it denies. In the hours the track counts a body
as up but the rounded numbers put its rim under the skyline (some of the 168 rounding hours), there is nothing to draw:
the name is shown, and for the Sun the answer says "under 1%".

### Stretching heights changes nothing that is hidden

"Stretch heights ×5" scales the whole scene along "up": every height above or below eye level (the tangent of the
elevation) is multiplied by five. Eye level stays put, straight up stays straight up, and a point of a disk and the
point of land in front of it stay on the same line from the eye, so the same part of each disk is covered, stretched or
not. The tests check that the stretch multiplies heights and undoes exactly, that a hill one degree up is drawn five
times as far from eye level, that nothing moves sideways, and that a disk becomes an oval five times as tall as wide.

### Limits

- **Not held on a real phone.** The motion sensor has not turned the view on a device: only readings played by hand,
  its ways of failing, and iOS's permission question in the simulator, which has no sensor.
- **No headset view.** Not built, because there was nothing here to check one with.
- **Frame times are one Mac's,** with a slowed processor standing in for a phone's. The graphics card was not slowed.
- **The land is a shape, not ground.** Its lighter rim under the top edge and the Sun's glare are ways of drawing. There
  are no shadows, no slopes, and nothing beyond the skyline. The glare fades with the track's share of the Sun's disk,
  so there is no glow after the Sun has gone.
- **Between two hours the disks slide in a straight line** from one hourly position to the next and come to rest on the
  hour's own; a drag along the time river goes straight there.
- **Earth's face is not the real one.** Its lit part is the phase seen from the site; which side faces the Moon, and the
  tilt of its axis, are not modelled (its north is drawn along the Moon's).
- It inherits everything above: the horizon is the pipeline's, with its 0.5° steps and its ridge-top gap against NASA's
  map (§2).


## 14. The range: every hour of 2026 to 2049 (2026-11-13)

The data covered every hour of 2027 to 2030. It now covers every hour from 1 January 2026 00:00 UTC to 31 December 2049
23:00 UTC: 210,384 hours at each site, and 288 months of each light grid. Whole calendar years, because the app is organised
by month and year; 2026 so that today is inside the data.

**How far NASA's files reach.** Read from each kernel the pipeline loads, with SPICE's own coverage calls
(`spkcov`, `pckcov`):

| Kernel | Covers (UTC) |
| --- | --- |
| `de440s.bsp`, positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon | 26 December 1849 to 21 January 2150 |
| `moon_pa_de421_1900-2050.bpc`, how the Moon is turned | 31 December 1899 to 31 December 2050, 23:58:51 |
| `naif0012.tls`, leap seconds | no end; its last leap second is 1 January 2017 (TAI − UTC = 37 s) |

added before then would move those times by a second each, a few seconds at most by 2049. The Sun moves about 0.00014° across the
lunar sky in a second, far below what an hourly step or a 0.01° rounding can show.

  Sun-over-Earth months and the four years of positions for custom points: identical files;

## 16. Distance to permanently shadowed ground (2026-11-14)

The app's first science measure: how far each listed site is from ground that direct sunlight never reaches, where
water ice could survive. `pipeline/scripts/make_shadow_distance.py` writes it to `web/public/data/shadow.json`.

**The map.** NASA GSFC's PGDA publishes LOLA-derived maps of permanently shadowed regions (product 69, the "LPSR"
files; Mazarico et al. 2011). Each pixel is flagged in permanent shadow or not. Three maps cover the south pole, each
finer and smaller than the last: 60 m a pixel south of 85° S, 120 m south of 75° S, 240 m south of 65° S (the maps of
the north pole stop at 65° N). The label reads: "a map of the permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) in the lunar South
Pole ... The values are binary, indicating whether a pixel is in permanent shadow (1) or not (0)."

**What is measured.** The great-circle distance on the Moon's 1737.4 km sphere from the site to the centre of a
shadowed pixel: along the reference sphere, not a route over the hills, and good to the pixel's size. Each site is read on
the finest map that holds it, and an answer counts only if it is nearer than that map's edge is. Two answers:

- **the nearest patch**, a shadowed pixel of any size. Near the pole this is often a speck: one or two 60 m pixels in a
  small crater. It is real shadow in NASA's map, and too small to plan science around;
- **the nearest region of at least 10 km²**, the size at which LROC's Permanently Shadowed Regions Atlas starts listing
  them (its smallest is 10.001 km²). This is the one a science team would name.

The 100% rows show the cell is looked up in the right place and the hours line up. Where it falls
short, the cause is the one §12 measured: the cell's middle is up to 54 m from the site (Connecting
Ridge), and with the Sun grazing the skyline that is enough to change the answer. June 2027 at
Connecting Ridge is the worst seen: the grid's verdict differs from the site's for 29% of landing
hours. So near a ridge in a month of low Sun, the layer and the panel are a guide to the ground
around a site, not the site's verdict. A finer grid round the ridge sites is what would close this.

**The smooth Moon, against every listed site's track.** The same script, for the picture that colors
the Moon away from the grid (`moonWash.ts`), tried every six hours. Away from the pole it agrees with
the track for 96.7% to 100% of landings (November 2026 and June 2027: the Apollo sites, Blue Ghost,
Chandrayaan-3, SLIM, Chang'e 4). Nearer the pole it is poorer and varies with the month: IM-1
Odysseus 87.5% and 95.8%, IM-2 Athena 92.5% and 99.2%, and at the four ridge sites 71.7% to 97.5%,
where the grid is used and not the smooth Moon.

**The slope.** The 30 m slope images the layer reads (JPEG) against each ridge site's recorded slope
(5 m baseline, with its error): 1.9° against 1.2° ± 2.5°, 4.2° against 5.1° ± 1.8°, 1.0° against
1.6° ± 1.7°, 0.6° against 1.1° ± 1.7°. Each is inside the recorded error. The two measure slope over
different lengths of ground, so they are not expected to match exactly.
